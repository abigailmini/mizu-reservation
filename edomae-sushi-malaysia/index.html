Edomae Sushi:
The 200-Year-Old Craft Behind Every Piece

Not all sushi is the same. Edomae is the original Tokyo tradition — where fish is aged, rice is engineered, and every piece of nigiri is a small act of precision. Here's what it means, and how Mizu honours it in PJ.

Most people's introduction to sushi is a conveyor belt or a supermarket tray. It's fine. It's also almost nothing like the dish that was invented in Edo — old Tokyo — nearly two centuries ago. That original style has a name: Edomae sushi (江戸前寿司), literally "in front of Edo," referring to the fish caught in Tokyo Bay.

Understanding Edomae changes how you eat sushi forever. This is the tradition Mizu Omakase is built on.

The Origin: Fast Food That Became Fine Art

In the 1800s, before refrigeration, Edo's sushi chefs faced a problem: how do you serve fish that stays safe and delicious through a Tokyo summer? Their answer became technique. They cured with salt, marinated in soy, aged for flavour, and simmered or vinegared — each method both preserving the fish and deepening its taste. Sushi began as street food for busy workers. The craft of making it well turned it into one of the world's great cuisines.

Edomae is that inheritance: preservation techniques, refined over generations, applied not out of necessity now but because they simply make fish taste better.

The Four Pillars of Edomae — and How Mizu Follows Them

熟成

Aging the fish (Neta)

Contrary to the "fresher is always better" myth, many fish improve with controlled aging — days for some, over a week for others. Aging breaks down proteins into amino acids, concentrating umami and softening texture. Mizu ages its fish deliberately, each species to its own ideal window, rather than serving everything straight from ice.

シャリ

Seasoned rice (Shari)

In Edomae, the rice is not a neutral base — it's half the dish. Shari is seasoned with a balance of rice vinegar, salt and sometimes red vinegar (akazu), and crucially served at body temperature. Mizu treats shari as an ingredient in its own right, tuned per piece so it dissolves the instant it meets the fish.

仕事

The chef's work (Shigoto)

Shigoto means the preparation before you ever sit down — curing, marinating, scoring, brushing with nikiri soy. It's invisible labour that defines Edomae. At Mizu you taste that work in every course, not just raw fish on rice.

握り

Hand-forming (Nigiri)

A master shapes nigiri in seconds with just enough pressure to hold together yet fall apart on the tongue — air pockets intact, never compressed. Served immediately, one piece at a time, at the counter. Machines can't replicate it. Mizu's nigiri is formed to order in front of you.

Edomae vs. Conveyor Belt Sushi

Conveyor Belt / Supermarket
  • Fish served chilled, straight off ice
  • Rice cold, dense, often over-vinegared
  • Machine-pressed or pre-made hours ahead
  • No aging, curing or marinating
  • Eaten whenever it circles past you
Edomae at Mizu
  • Fish aged and cured to its ideal peak
  • Shari at body temperature, tuned per piece
  • Hand-formed to order at the counter
  • Days of quiet preparation behind each piece
  • Served the second it's made, in sequence

Both are "sushi." Only one is a craft you can taste. When you search for traditional or authentic sushi in KL and PJ, Edomae is the word you're really looking for.

How to Eat Edomae Nigiri

  • Eat it immediately. Nigiri is engineered to be perfect for about ten seconds. Don't let it sit.
  • Skip the soy dunk. The chef has already seasoned each piece with nikiri or salt. Extra soy drowns it.
  • Fish-side down if you do dip — never soak the rice.
  • One bite if you can. Edomae nigiri is sized to be eaten whole, so the rice-to-fish ratio lands exactly as intended.
  • Trust the order. The chef builds from light to rich for a reason. Follow the progression.
